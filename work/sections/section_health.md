## Health, Diet & Fitness

### Running and Exercise Routines
Getting into an exercise habit doesn't have to mean an hour at the gym: a daily 20-minute plan built with input from trainers and fitness experts promises to keep the body active and growing stronger without heavy equipment, varying the routine day to day to stave off boredom[^1]. For people who'd rather be walked through a routine than plan one themselves, the Sworkit Pro app generates randomized workouts tailored to however much time is available and which muscle groups a person wants to target, talking users through each exercise so they don't need to keep glancing at a screen, while also tracking progress and offering video demonstrations[^2].

Running in particular comes up repeatedly as an accessible, low-cost way to start a fitness routine. One piece addresses the specific fear of being "bad" at running—the kind of out-of-breath, doubled-over experience that keeps people from lacing up in the first place—and lays out how to begin anyway[^3]. Another, written by a Lifehacker staffer reflecting on her own return to training after running the NYC marathon, argues that the real secret to becoming a runner is building a consistent routine, so that pulling on running shoes stops being something to think twice about[^4]. Not every path marketed as a fitness shortcut holds up, though: time in a sauna can feel good, but on its own it isn't a substitute for exercise, and it doesn't detox the body or melt away fat[^5].

### Muscle Recovery and Body Mechanics
Some exercises earn their keep specifically by protecting against pain elsewhere in the body: the bird dog exercise works the stabilizer muscles in the core, encouraging people to move from the hips rather than the lower back, which helps reduce lower back pain—even though it looks deceptively easy and is commonly performed with poor form[^6]. Understanding what happens after a hard workout matters too. The soreness that sets in a day or two later, known as delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), isn't caused by lactic acid or toxins, as is commonly assumed; if it were, it would show up during the workout itself rather than hours or days afterward, and exactly what does cause it is still not fully understood[^7]. In the moment, a different kind of muscle pain—a sudden cramp, like the calf cramp that wakes someone up at night or stops them mid-workout—can often be released by contracting the opposing muscle, such as pulling the toes upward to counter a calf cramp, rather than stretching the cramping muscle itself[^8].

### Mental Health and Stress Management
Managing stress sometimes comes down to a shift in perspective. Jerry Seinfeld has described calming his nerves during high-pressure moments in the Seinfeld writers' room by looking at Hubble Space Telescope photographs, using the vastness of space to remind himself how insignificant any single stressful moment really is[^9]. Screen habits factor into mental health too: researchers at Ohio State University Medical Center linked late-night sessions in front of a glowing screen in the dark to changes in behavior and the brain resembling depression, based on how hamsters responded to dim, unnatural light at night, suggesting that either stepping away from the computer or turning on the lights during those sessions could help[^10].

### Pandemic-Era Health and Safety
The COVID-19 pandemic reshaped both the stresses people faced and the scams built to exploit them. With burnout described as "chronic stress gone awry" by psychologist Sheryl Ziegler, the nonstop barrage of pandemic-era stressors pushed many people toward emotional, mental, and physical exhaustion, prompting guidance on how to recognize and head off burnout during that period[^11]. At the same time, the pandemic became fodder for cybercriminals: the Microsoft Security Intelligence Team disclosed two large coronavirus-themed phishing campaigns that tricked people into downloading malicious Excel files disguised as legitimate spreadsheets, granting hackers remote access to victims' PCs[^12].

### Everyday Health Tips and Quick Fixes
Smaller, practical fixes round out the picture. Blocked sinuses—made extra stressful during the pandemic, when any sniffle raised the question of allergies versus COVID—can sometimes be relieved in about 20 seconds using an acupressure trick involving the face and tongue, offering an alternative to reaching for a pill[^13]. Excessive sweating, meanwhile, can have a genetic component according to dermatologist Michele Green, but there are still ways to manage it even for people who sweat heavily in everyday situations like job interviews[^14]. And health isn't only a matter of the human body: Apple updated its AppleCare terms so that batteries in iPhones, iPads, iPods, Apple Watches, and Macs became eligible for replacement once they fell below 80% of their original capacity, a looser threshold than the previous 50% cutoff that had applied to iOS devices[^15].
