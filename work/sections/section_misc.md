## Miscellaneous Lifehacks

### Quick Physical Tricks and DIY Fixes
A lot of the grab-bag tips in this collection are small physical maneuvers that solve an everyday annoyance with almost no materials. The microwave, it turns out, is more versatile than most people give it credit for — a roundup of tricks covers steaming vegetables in a covered bowl with a splash of water, among other uses that go beyond simple reheating[^1]. For photos, ditching the word "cheese" in favor of "cheeks" is offered as a way to produce a more natural, Duchenne-style smile, since the "k" sound shapes the mouth and cheeks into a more genuine expression[^2]. Glasses-wearers get a fix for perpetual fogging: rubbing a small amount of shaving cream on the lenses, a trick originally shared for cold-weather mirror fogging that took on new relevance once face masks became an everyday accessory[^3]. When it comes to opening a bottle with no opener in sight, hammering a construction nail into a piece of wood at roughly a 45-degree angle creates an improvised bottle opener, as long as the nail head is wide enough to grip the cap[^4]. Hanging art evenly is another common headache solved with an ordinary object — sliding a fork onto the nail, with the handle angled upward and the tines gripping the shaft, gives the picture wire a stable perch to hook onto so the piece hangs straight without any measuring[^5]. Doors that slam can be quieted (though not fully silenced) by sticking felt pads inside the door frame near the latch or along the top and bottom edges, catching the door before it hits the frame[^6].

Several of these fixes lean further into DIY territory. A functional tablet stylus can be built from an arrowhead pencil eraser, a metal paintbrush, and some craft foam to hold the pieces together[^7]. On the more ambitious end, a serviceable air purifier can be built for around $30 by taping a MERV-13-rated furnace filter to a 20-inch box fan, a setup demonstrated to remove roughly 90 percent of the smoke, dust, and allergen particles a much pricier HEPA purifier would catch[^8]. For looking sharp instead of rumpled, the "military tuck" involves pinching the excess fabric at a shirt's side seams and folding it back before tucking it in, producing a slimmer line than a plain tuck[^9]. And when shipping something breakable, marking the box "glass" or "liquid" is suggested as more effective than "fragile," since handlers reportedly respond to the concrete threat of a mess to clean up rather than a vague warning label[^10].

### Safety, Prep, and Everyday Maintenance
A handful of items are about being ready for ordinary mishaps rather than performing a trick. A graphic on tire safety lays out how to check tread depth with a coin, when to rotate tires, how to read a tire's sidewall, and where to check inflation, all framed as routine maintenance that affects both gas mileage and accident risk[^11]. In a similar vein, an infographic designed to be saved to a phone or printed and kept in a wallet walks through what to do in the event of an emergency — such as getting pulled over, being mugged, or being in an accident — on the logic that this kind of guidance is only useful if it's on hand when needed[^12]. Paper records get their own cautionary note: scanning receipts into a document-management service is a good way to keep them backed up, but it's worth holding onto the originals for a while too, since some stores and store employees have been known to refuse a photocopied receipt for a return even when a photo on a phone eventually persuades them[^13]. On the housekeeping side, a roundup of chores that only need to happen about once a year — as opposed to the daily grind of dishes and floors — is offered as a small consolation for anyone tracking a home-maintenance list[^14].

### Communication and Social Savvy
Several items focus on the small verbal and behavioral choices that shape how other people perceive you. Borrowing a rule from improv comedy, one tip suggests that when a conversation stalls, falling back on the "history/philosophy/metaphor" approach — bringing up the history of a topic, a broader philosophical angle, or an apt metaphor — can keep a scene, and a conversation, moving instead of stalling into awkward silence[^15]. Relatedly, instead of reflexively answering "I'm busy" when asked how you've been, a productivity author argues that busyness has become a kind of humble-brag rather than a real indicator of anything, and that naming what you're actually accomplishing lands better than flaunting how overloaded you are[^16]. A study on written communication found that including a middle initial — as in "David F. Clark" rather than "David Clark" — measurably increased how intelligent and high-status readers judged a piece of writing to be, though the effect only showed up in settings tied to intellectual performance[^17]. Typing habits reveal generational history too: people taught to type on a typewriter were generally told to put two spaces after a period, but a career counselor's advice frames single-spacing as the modern standard, warning that the old habit can read as an age signal on a resume or in professional emails[^18].

Workplace and social dynamics get their own set of tips. Heading into a job interview, it's suggested that candidates come prepared with their own questions about a prospective employer's culture rather than only fielding questions from the hiring side, since the interview format can otherwise turn into something closer to a one-sided pitch[^19]. For anyone in a position to impose change on other people — a manager redirecting a team, or a parent moving a family — leaving room for some kind of choice within the mandatory shift, even a small one, is described as a way to make people feel like participants in the change rather than subjects of it[^20]. And one more pointed workplace hack: creating an email persona for a fictitious male assistant, a workaround one writer's friend used after growing tired of being labeled "difficult" or "bossy" for tasks that read differently when a man's name is attached to them[^21].

### Mindset, Motivation, and Self-Improvement
A few entries are less about a discrete action and more about reframing how to think about setbacks, personality, and time. One roundup collects stories of well-known people's biggest professional failures and how they recovered from them, framed as a reminder that failure is a normal part of any path to success rather than a sign that a career is over[^22]. Relatedly, a piece on channeling one's "dark side" argues that traits like envy don't have to be purely destructive — distinguishing "benign" envy, which reflects your own aspirations, from "malicious" envy, is offered as a first step toward turning a negative feeling into motivation or insight[^23]. A podcast conversation with journalist Celeste Headlee, author of "Do Nothing," tackles the broader culture of busyness and overwork, discussing why so many people end up working more rather than less even while at home, and what that does to both mind and body[^24]. And for anyone trying to get through more reading in less time, the "third word rule" proposes skipping the first and last couple of words on each line, relying on peripheral vision to still catch the content — a technique said to work even better when text is formatted into narrower columns[^25].

### Digital Tools and Online Resources
A large cluster of items are pointers to specific sites, services, and tools rather than techniques. For anyone in need of non-stock-looking photography, a roundup rounds up more than 30 free image sites, many offering Creative Commons Zero licensing so the images can be reused without restriction[^26]. On the privacy side, a guide to opting out of popular people-search sites like Whitepages and Spokeo walks through how to get personal information — addresses, family members, known associates — pulled from those databases[^27]. Reader polls and roundups cover recommended tools for everyday needs: a callout for the most trusted weather websites for checking forecasts, hurricanes, and pollen counts[^28], and a shortlist of the best sites for printing custom business cards based on reader nominations[^29]. For people who'd rather not hand their data to Gmail, Feedly, or similar services, a roundup of self-hosted alternatives argues that running your own version of a service, while more work upfront, offers more control over your data and insulates you from a company shutting the service down[^30]. In the same spirit, Just Delete Me is described as a single site collecting direct links to the account-deletion pages of many popular web services, meant to make the process of closing out unwanted accounts less of a scavenger hunt[^31]. On the more educational end, Mozilla's Web Literacy Map lays out beginner-friendly activities meant to teach essential modern skills like navigating the web, writing code, and engaging with others online[^32], while a separate roundup of the top 10 tools for a free online education highlights massive open online courses (MOOCs) from universities and other providers as a way to keep learning without paying tuition[^33]. For digital paperwork specifically, the service FileThis is described as pulling together electronic statements from a wide range of financial institutions into a single online filing cabinet, similar in spirit to Mint but focused on document collection rather than budgeting[^34].

A few items step back to explain how certain everyday technology actually works, or reflect on Lifehacker itself. One piece walks through why progress bars are so often wrong, explaining that a computer has too many unknown variables to accurately estimate how long a download or process will take, and that a progress bar is still more useful than no indicator at all even when its estimate is off[^35]. A short video is offered as a beginner-friendly explanation of what "life hacking" actually means — not the movie-style image of breaking into government systems, but simply finding a clever way around an established system to make life easier[^36] — echoing the site's own FAQ, which describes Lifehacker as "self-help for power users" covering software, web sites, DIY projects, and shortcuts for going beyond default settings[^37].

### Gear and Being Prepared On the Go
Two items round up what to actually carry with you day to day. A reader-submitted "go bag" post breaks down the contents of one organized student's messenger bag, which included a laptop, a phone, noise-cancelling headphones, a fitness tracker, sunglasses, a slim wallet, and a small organizer pouch for cables, chargers, and allergy medicine[^38]. In a similar but more general vein, a top-10 list of essential wallet, keychain, and pocket tools focuses on small, discreet items meant to keep a person prepared without weighing them down with a bulky kit[^39].

### Food, Fun, Novelty, and Travel
This grab-bag rounds out with lighter, more social items. A list of Trader Joe's products argues that a dozen of the chain's private-label goods actually beat their name-brand counterparts, framed within the store's broader reputation for a quirky, less corporate grocery experience[^40]. Sticking with food-as-social-currency, showing up to a potluck or gathering with a bag of fast-food cheeseburgers is framed as a nearly foolproof crowd-pleaser that no one is ever upset to see arrive[^41]. On the money-back side, a class-action settlement against Red Bull once allowed anyone who had purchased the drink between 2002 and 2014 to claim either product or a small cash payout without needing proof of purchase, tied to allegations that the company misrepresented the drink's functional benefits[^42]. For venting about bad parking, a website called You Park Like an Asshole offered downloadable notices that could be printed and left on offending cars, letting frustrated drivers register their disapproval without a confrontation[^43]. And for travelers, learning a destination's local slang — beyond just the basics of the language — is presented as another way to have a more authentic experience in cities like Paris, Rome, London, Berlin, Boston, and Shanghai[^44].

### Year-End Roundups and Listicles
A handful of items are themselves roundups collecting other tips, included here because the roundup format was the item rather than a single standalone hack. A collection of the site's most popular guest posts of the year features outside contributors' advice, including a widely read suggestion that sticking to exactly two drinks at a party is a reasonable default for people without a strong personal rule already[^45]. Two separate year-end wrap-ups gather the most popular personal finance posts of their respective years, covering ground like airfare booking timing and what's worth buying at Costco[^46], and broader guides, primers, and explainers on money management aimed at readers trying to spend less than they earn and build up savings[^47]. Rounding out the format, a top-10 list of things people might be doing wrong every day covers small daily habits — starting, only half-jokingly, with the mechanics of breathing itself — arguing that even basic routine actions often have a more efficient version[^48].

### COVID-Era Health and Social Notes
Two items reflect the pandemic-specific advice that circulated during 2020. "Quaran-teaming," or forming a "quarantine bubble" between two isolated households, is discussed as a way to combine resources — sharing child care, or helping an elderly relative who's struggling alone — while trying to limit the added exposure risk as much as possible[^49]. Alongside that, an explainer on contact tracing describes the practice as a long-used public health tool, previously applied to diseases like HIV and tuberculosis, aimed at identifying people who may have been exposed to an infection and helping contain its spread as the country navigated reopening[^50].
