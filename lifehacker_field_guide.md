## Productivity & Career

### Job Search Strategy

Landing a job often comes down to strategy as much as qualifications. One counterintuitive tactic is to resist the urge to apply the moment a listing goes up — Wise Bread's advice, relayed on Lifehacker, is to let a job ad "run its course" and send an application a few weeks later, which can make a candidate appear more deliberate and less desperate than the flood of same-day applicants[^1]. Once you're ready to apply, having the right tools matters: a roundup of ten job-search tools starts with the basics, pointing to free resume templates from Google Docs and Microsoft Word, or a fill-in-the-blank builder for anyone whose resume just needs a layout refresh — though the specific builder the roundup named, ineedaresu.me, is now a defunct/parked domain, so look for a similar free template site instead[^2]. After the application goes out, it's common to second-guess every detail — did you spell the hiring manager's name right, should you have said more — but The Muse argues that most of these post-submission worries aren't actually the mistakes job seekers fear they are[^3]. And when a prospective employer offers unpaid work "for exposure" instead of pay, Refinery29's advice is to ask them to quantify exactly what that exposure means, since it's really a form of advertising and can be measured in clicks, page views, or reach[^4].

### Resumes and Cover Letters

A resume is a marketing document, and several old Lifehacker posts focus on making it sharper. One reader question captures the core problem directly: how do you sell your skills beyond a "boring, old paper resume," using web apps to build something more eye-catching than a static document[^5]. On content, the advice is generally to cut rather than add — an old internship or a job you were fired from may not need to be there at all, though exactly what to leave off varies depending on which career counselor you ask[^6]. The career-objective statement at the top of a resume is also considered outdated by at least one recruiting expert, who suggests replacing it with a qualifications summary that speaks directly to the specific job opening[^7]. When accomplishments don't come with obvious hard numbers, The Daily Muse recommends quantifying them anyway using range, frequency, or scale — for example, turning "supervised undergraduate students" into "supervised 7-12 undergraduate students per year"[^8]. Another way to sharpen a resume is to trade drafts with a colleague: comparing how someone else describes a shared project or the company you both work for can surface better phrasing, supporting numbers, or context you hadn't thought to include[^9]. For cover letters, the Glassdoor Blog suggests skipping the generic "Dear sir or madam" opener in favor of a mini anecdote relevant to the company, or writing as though you already have the job — essentially treating the letter like an acceptance speech to project confidence[^10]. 

### Building Your Network and LinkedIn Presence

Staying visible on LinkedIn without spending hours on it is its own skill. Career development specialist Lily Zhang's three-pronged strategy divides LinkedIn activity into daily, weekly, and monthly tasks, so a job seeker or professional can maintain a steady, active-looking presence with a modest time investment[^12]. The LinkedIn summary itself benefits from a clear call to action — The Daily Muse recommends explicitly telling profile visitors what you want them to do next, rather than leaving your summary as a passive bio[^13]. Off the platform, making a strong impression in person doesn't require being the loudest person in the room; the key to being interesting when meeting new people is simply showing up and finding conversations on topics you're genuinely passionate about, letting the interest come across naturally rather than forcing it[^14].

### Acing the Interview

Several posts zero in on specific interview moments that can make or break an impression. Nearly every interview ends with some version of "Do you have any questions for us?" — a question interviewers use to judge a candidate's priorities, and one that leaves a bad impression if answered with a flat "no"[^15]. Word choice matters throughout the conversation, too: Real Simple's advice is to strip out qualifying hedges like "just," "actually," and "kinda," since these habits make a candidate sound less confident and less authoritative than they mean to be[^16]. A related trap is dwelling on why you left your last job — a College Atlas survey of over 2,000 hiring managers found that over-explaining a past departure, or seeming not over it, ranked among the top interview turn-offs, so the recommended approach is a short, prepared answer and moving on[^17]. To have strong answers ready for the many behavioral questions interviewers ask, career expert Liz Ryan suggests preparing a set of 15 personal stories in advance, since storytelling tends to be more memorable than reciting straight facts[^18]. After the interview, a thank-you note — emailed or handwritten — can help a candidate stand out from the field, and it's worth sending even after a rejection, since a company may circle back if the person they hired doesn't work out or a new position opens[^19].

### Negotiating Offers and Handling Criticism

Once an offer or a difficult conversation arrives, how you respond shapes what happens next. The Muse cautions against accepting a job based on salary alone without getting the full offer in writing, since agreeing too quickly can leave you with little leverage to negotiate other details, like short-term disability insurance, later on[^20]. Criticism from a boss is easier to navigate productively when you start your response with "what" instead of "why" — productivity consultant Garrett Miller, quoted in The Wall Street Journal, notes that "what" questions like "What evidence did you see?" draw out more constructive, specific feedback than "why" questions tend to[^21].

### Workplace Communication and Meetings

Bad meetings are a common complaint, and one piece breaks down how to avoid running one: before scheduling anything, address the three Ws — when, who, and why the meeting needs to happen at all — since skipping the "why" is often what makes meetings a waste of time[^22]. Some of that time can be reclaimed by automating the status update itself; the free web service Teamreporter collects information from everyone on a team, condenses it into a single digest, and emails it out automatically, cutting down on meetings held just to get everyone up to speed[^23]. For workplace debates and disagreements, the "steel man" tactic offers a more constructive alternative to the common "straw man" fallacy: rather than arguing against a weakened, distorted version of someone's position, a steel man argument means finding and engaging with the strongest possible version of the other side's point[^24].

### Email and Task Management Tools

A handful of posts round up tools for staying on top of email and to-do lists. When spam threatens to overwhelm an inbox, the recommended countermeasures work across webmail and desktop clients alike, though the original 2010 post singled out Gmail as having especially effective spam filtering at the time — a comparison that's since been overtaken, as most major webmail providers have significantly improved their filtering[^25]. On the sending side, the Chrome extension MailTrack adds simple read receipts to Gmail by embedding a small tracking image in outgoing messages, offering an easier alternative to more complex email-tracking tools[^26]. For task management, TodoTxtMac brings a graphical interface to Gina Trapani's plain-text Todo.txt system on the Mac, keeping the advantages of a plain-text to-do list while making it easier to manage day to day — though the original free, open-source app hasn't seen regular updates since around 2017, with its developer's attention now on a paid successor, SwiftoDo Desktop[^27]. The Projecteze system takes a different approach entirely, requiring nothing more than a word processor: a four-column table — Project, Priority, Dates, and Commitments & Action Items — lets you track projects and break tasks into smaller, dated pieces without adopting any new software[^28].

### Habits, Focus, and Career Growth

Building better habits, understanding how work time is actually spent, and finding room for growth outside a primary job round out this set of articles. James Clear, author of Atomic Habits, argues that setting goals is counterproductive compared to building the right habits, and lays out four rules for making a new habit stick[^29]. Once at work, it turns out a meaningful chunk of the day may go to "cyberloafing" rather than actual tasks — an Atlantic piece cited on Lifehacker frames this alongside a broader trend of work intensifying even as burnout and stress rise[^30]. On the mental side, distraction — something as simple as playing Sudoku — can be more useful than forcing "positive thinking," since there's value in actually experiencing difficult emotions rather than only trying to talk yourself out of them[^31]. Outside of core job duties, the Buffer blog makes the case that creative side projects, whether a blog, a book, a newsletter, or photography, improve other areas of life even if they never turn into the next big company[^32]. For those considering an advanced degree, a list of things people wish they'd known before starting grad school notes that in many fields it's all but required, or at least useful for standing out from other candidates[^33]. And for anyone traveling for work, it's worth packing your home state's highway toll transponder rather than paying a rental car company's fee for theirs — systems like E-ZPass often work across many states, covering much of the East Coast and reaching as far as Illinois via the compatible I-Pass system[^34].

## Finance & Money

### Taxes
Tax season brings its share of surprises, and not always pleasant ones. When the New York Times published a deep investigative report on the Trump family's tax practices, drawing on more than 100,000 documents including lawsuits, financial paperwork, and depositions, it detailed how the family allegedly set up a sham corporation and used other schemes to help move hundreds of millions of dollars while minimizing tax liability[^35]. On a more everyday level, the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 lowered rates and doubled the standard deduction, leading most filers to expect bigger refunds — but the IRS itself warned that a larger-than-usual share of filers who normally got refunds would instead owe money, and some would face penalties[^36]. Keeping old tax records handy matters too: the IRS's Get Transcript service lets filers download years of past tax return transcripts directly, a major improvement over the previous process, which required a paper questionnaire and a 5-10 business day wait[^37]. Taxes can also factor into unexpected purchases — booking a wedding at a museum, park, or other space owned by a non-profit instead of a traditional banquet hall can cut costs and may even come with a tax deduction[^38]. And for married couples managing student debt, the cited article recommends filing taxes as "Married Filing Separately" rather than jointly for someone on an Income-Based Repayment plan, since IBR bases the monthly payment on individual rather than joint income — a strategy that can still apply today, though student loan repayment rules have grown more complex since this was written (IBR remains available, but SAVE ended in 2026 and a new Repayment Assistance Plan has its own rules), so borrowers should check the tradeoff against their current plan before filing separately[^39].

### Student Loans and Debt Repayment
Paying down debt faster than the minimum requires some strategy. One approach for student loan borrowers is to request extra principal-only payments, but that only works well if the loan servicer is willing to "ungroup" separate loans so a borrower can target extra payments at a specific high-interest loan rather than having them spread across a bundled group — worth noting that several servicers named in the original article, including Navient, FedLoan Servicing, and Great Lakes, have since exited federal student loan servicing (Great Lakes' accounts moved to Nelnet), so check with your current servicer, such as Nelnet, MOHELA, Aidvantage, or EdFinancial, about whether this is possible[^40]. Geography can matter too: because state income tax rates vary widely, relocating can meaningfully change how much a borrower pays toward their loan over time, as illustrated by a Student Loan Hero calculator and the story of the company's CEO, who moved from New York City to income-tax-free Austin, Texas[^41]. Once a big debt — whether a car loan, student loan, or credit card balance — is finally paid off, the newly freed-up discretionary income calls for a plan, starting with steps to avoid sliding back into debt[^42]. For borrowers whose credit has already taken a hit from missed payments, a "goodwill letter" offers a way to ask a lender or servicer to remove a reported late payment and restore an account to good standing, a tactic typically aimed at those who missed payments due to a temporary financial hardship[^43].

### Credit Scores and Credit Cards
Monitoring and protecting credit standing takes some know-how. Consumers are legally entitled to a free credit report via AnnualCreditReport.com — originally once a year, though the three bureaus made free weekly reports permanent in 2023 — though that service doesn't include an actual credit score; a growing number of companies now offer free access to various credit scores through other channels, though it pays to watch out for credit-score scams[^44]. Just as important as building credit is knowing what can quietly damage it — beyond the obvious issue of missed payments, maxing out available credit hurts the "debt utilization ratio" that credit bureaus use in scoring, and Credit Karma recommends keeping usage under 30 percent of the total limit[^45]. Credit cards can also come with lesser-known perks: some card issuers offer damage protection for cellphones when the monthly phone bill is paid with an eligible card, with Wells Fargo and First Citizens Bank among the more prominent examples out of more than 25 banks and credit unions reviewed[^46]. Card security matters in person, too — travelers can ask a hotel for an "Attestation of Compliance" certificate confirming PCI compliance, the standard of security checks credit card companies require of businesses, since surveys have found some hotel managers unaware they even need to meet the standard[^47]. Credit risk isn't purely individual, either: marrying someone with existing debt can create liability questions, and understanding when a spouse's financial history can affect a joint credit standing is an important step in protecting one's own credit history as a fallback[^48].

### Earning Extra Income
For those looking to boost their income, there's no shortage of paths — from the "super-lazy to the intensely-engaged" — for making extra money in spare time[^49]. An infographic compiling over 200 online resources breaks these options down further, covering freelancing as a writer, designer, or programmer; affiliate and CPA marketing; adding content to network sites; producing videos; and flipping web properties[^50]. Earning more from an existing job is its own skill, and negotiation research suggests that job candidates who set the "anchor" by naming a number first in a salary negotiation tend to come out ahead — and, according to a University of Idaho psychological scientist's research, even framing that number as part of a joke can nudge an employer's ultimate offer higher rather than lower[^51]. When a job doesn't work out and an employer requests a resignation rather than issuing a firing, that moment can double as a negotiating opportunity: since resigning normally disqualifies someone from unemployment benefits, getting the employer to agree in writing not to contest an unemployment claim can preserve that income source[^52]. And for a sense of where personal income stacks up regionally, an analysis using the Pew Charitable Trust's definition of "middle class" — households earning between 67 and 200 percent of a state's median income — combined with U.S. Census data produced state-by-state salary figures showing the middle class had shrunk in every state over the prior decade or so[^53].

### Smart Everyday Spending
Small tactical choices can add up to real savings on everyday purchases. At a restaurant, asking the sommelier for the "bin end list" or "pocket list" can turn up wine that's discounted because the bottle is scuffed or the restaurant is trying to clear out a vintage that's being phased out or is nearly sold through[^54]. Timing matters for bigger purchases as well — the cited article recommends booking airfare on Tuesday or Wednesday, and specifically in the early morning hours, such as around 1am, when demand from other shoppers is thought to be lowest, though later airline-pricing research has found little consistent day-of-week or time-of-day advantage, with how far in advance you book mattering more[^55].

### Retirement and Long-Term Planning
Planning ahead extends beyond day-to-day budgeting. Choosing between a Traditional and a Roth IRA largely comes down to the timing of taxation: contributions to a traditional IRA are tax-deductible up front but distributions are taxed on withdrawal, while Roth contributions aren't deductible but withdrawals of contributions can be accessed differently, a distinction a Charles Schwab infographic lays out for savers weighing the two account types[^56]. Planning for a future where someone may become incapacitated is equally important, and appointing a trusted financial power of attorney agent — someone authorized to manage another person's financial affairs if they're no longer able to — is one of the most common ways people prepare for that possibility, particularly for those aging independently[^57].

### Money and Relationships
Money habits don't always align between partners, and reconciling them can take years. One writer described how she and her partner started out with starkly different spending styles — he tended to spend a work bonus or raise immediately, while she was more conservative — and detailed how the couple worked through the resulting friction to eventually coexist without the fighting that once accompanied their financial differences[^58].

### Consumer Protections
Financial fine print carries consequences beyond the specific product it's attached to. When Dropbox updated its terms of service to include forced arbitration — while also offering a way to opt out — the move drew attention to why forced arbitration clauses are worth avoiding whenever a company provides an opt-out option, a practice increasingly common across various service agreements[^59].

## Shopping & Deals

### Timing Your Purchases
A bit of planning about *when* to buy can save as much money as hunting for coupons. Lifehacker published a comprehensive guide breaking down the best time of year to buy everything from airfare to appliances, based on when stores are clearing old stock, dropping prices ahead of holidays, or simply running seasonal sales; seasonal pricing calendars like this one shift over time, so it's worth checking for a newer version before relying on specific months[^60]. An earlier version of the same guide covered similar ground, recommending furniture purchases early in the year, laptops in April, and appliances in September to catch off-season, inventory-moving prices[^61].

### Clever Frugal Tricks
Sometimes the fastest way to a lower price is changing what you call the item you're searching for. A Reddit thread on r/Frugal notes that gym mats turn up cheaper when searched as "horse stall mats," and that a medical folding wedge can stand in for a pricier specialty wedge—though commenters caution that prices shift enough that each substitution is worth double-checking before relying on it[^62]. A more physical trick repurposes an old, otherwise-useless gift card as a small spool for a strip of duct tape, so a bit of tape is always on hand for repairs, blisters, or other on-the-go fixes, the same way campers wrap tape around a water bottle[^63].

### Price Matching and Smart Online Shopping
At the time of this 2014 roundup, most major retailers had price-match policies, and retail analysts found that only about 5% of consumers bothered asking a store to match a lower price they'd found elsewhere; the roundup laid out six major retailers' specifics, but price-matching has narrowed significantly since then — Target ended its policy entirely in 2025, and Walmart's matching is internal-only — so shoppers should confirm a retailer's current policy directly rather than relying on this list[^64]. 

### Freebies, Coupons, and Rewards
A crowdsourced master list compiled by the blog Couponers United rounded up companies—including brands like Burt's Bees—that would mail out coupons, and sometimes free products, to anyone who simply emailed and asked with a mailing address; company policies like this change often, so it's worth confirming a given brand still offers it before emailing[^66]. Many retailers and restaurants also hand out freebies, coupons, or discounts around a customer's birthday, and a roundup chart collected these offers from major chains as of its publication; birthday-club perks change frequently, so it's worth checking a chain's current loyalty program before counting on a specific offer[^67].

### Grocery Shopping Strategies
Buying dairy in bulk from warehouse clubs like Costco or Sam's Club can cut costs, but smaller households risk letting the extra spoil; the fix is to freeze the excess milk, pouring off enough beforehand to leave room for expansion in the jug[^68]. Thorin Klosowski streamlined his own grocery routine—which he considers a chore—using a mix of automated delivery, simple meal planning, and a handful of in-store tactics to get every trip down to under 30 minutes[^69]. And for shoppers who care about dough-to-ice-cream ratio, a taste test that melted down and strained several brands of cookie dough ice cream identified which pints deliver the most dough per bite[^70].

### Travel Deals
Beyond standard online travel agencies like Expedia, Orbitz, and Travelocity, deal-hunters could find "first dibs" on discounts by following airlines on Twitter (rebranded X in 2023), where carriers sometimes shared last-minute airfare deals that never made it to the OTAs; airlines' social-media deal habits have shifted since then, so it's worth checking whichever platforms a given carrier currently uses[^71]. Cruise shoppers have their own seasonal window during the Cruise Lines International Association's annual Cruise Week, when lines such as AMA Waterways and Uniworld have offered stackable incentives that can be combined with other promotions already on offer, plus extras some travel agents throw in themselves; the event still runs yearly but has more recently landed in September rather than October, so check CLIA's current schedule for exact dates[^72].

### Big Purchases and Buyer Beware
For anyone shopping for a used car, Popular Mechanics put together a two-page checklist that color-codes potential issues from green (not serious) to blue (deal breaker), letting buyers tally the results against a scorecard to judge whether a given car is worth the money[^73]. Gift cards carry their own risks: hidden fees and a patchwork of state and federal rules mean the balance isn't always as reliable as it looks, which is why cash is generally the safer bet for gift-giving[^74]. And before committing to a phone purchase, it's possible to virtualize Android on a PC to try out a device's software experience ahead of buying[^75].

## Home, DIY & Life Skills

### Cleaning and Household Upkeep
Some parts of the house get cleaned constantly, while others quietly get ignored for months or years. A visual guide from housewares provider Moshells lays out a schedule for exactly those commonly neglected spots, recommending that garbage disposals get cleaned out once a week, bath mats get washed every two weeks, indoor trash cans get rinsed every couple of months, and window screens get tidied up once a year, along with guidance for things like pet bowls[^76]. For everyday dusting, you don't need disposable Swiffer-style cloths — an old t-shirt cut into pieces and soaked in a glass container with hot water, two tablespoons of olive oil, and the juice of two lemons makes a reusable, pre-treated dust-trapping cloth that can be washed and used again and again[^77]. Cast iron care comes with a persistent myth that soap will strip a pan's seasoning, but Serious Eats' Kenji debunked this by pointing out that seasoning isn't just a layer of oil sitting on the surface — it's polymerized oil that has chemically bonded to the pan through repeated heating, so ordinary dish soap won't damage it[^78]. Pastry brushes need similar mythbusting-adjacent attention: they're used for egg washes, mustard on lamb, basting roasts, and coating a whole chicken in mayonnaise, which means they get quite gunky, and while silicone versions are easier to clean, bristle brushes remain the preferred tool despite the extra scrubbing[^79].

### Organizing Your Space (and Digital Life)
Getting organized, physically or digitally, tends to come down to a plan rather than a single trick. For syncing services like Dropbox, the real work is splitting the problem into two parts: organization, which is mostly on you, and synchronization, which Dropbox handles automatically once your folder structure makes sense across Windows, Mac, and Linux machines[^80]. For a more systematic approach to decluttering an entire home, a tour through Julie Morgenstern's bestselling book "Organizing from the Inside Out" walks through forming a genuine plan of attack for lifelong organization, rather than a one-time cleanup[^81]. Once you're organized, keeping categories visually distinct matters too — Kenneth L. Kelly's "22 colors of maximum contrast" chart offers a ranked list of colors that stay easy to tell apart from each other, so if you're color-coding five calendars you'd use the first five colors on the list, and if you have fourteen, the first fourteen[^82]. On the digital side, Mac users don't have to drag a file out of the Trash just to check what it is — tapping the space bar while a file is selected in the Trash opens Quick Look right there, letting you preview it without moving it first[^83]. And when organizing spills over into actually getting rid of things, several organizations exist specifically to pick up furniture and other household items you no longer want, letting you clear out clutter without hauling it anywhere yourself[^84].

### DIY Projects and Repairs
A number of small projects make home upkeep and building easier with tools you likely already have. A basic laser printer can do more than print on plain paper — one method for making your own labels and decals involves printing text onto paper, covering that area with clear packing tape, and using a bowl of lukewarm water to transfer the design onto the tape, giving you a functional label without a dedicated label maker[^85]. When it's time to hang something on the wall, you don't need a dedicated stud finder either: since most screws are magnetic and studs commonly have screws in them from construction, running a magnet along the wall can reveal stud locations just as effectively[^86]. Bigger materials present their own challenges — a standard 4'x8' sheet of plywood weighs anywhere from 25 to 85 pounds and is awkward for one person to carry, but tying a 20-foot length of rope into a loop and hooking it around two corners of the sheet makes it manageable solo[^87]. Outdoor spaces get some attention too: painting rocks with glow-in-the-dark paint creates a glowing guide along a dark driveway or walkway, an idea especially handy around Halloween[^88], and an adjustable outdoor bench can be built in about an hour using twelve cinder blocks and four 4x4 wooden planks, sanded and varnished (with moisture-resistant woods like redwood or cedar recommended) — the original roughly-$40 materials estimate dates to 2014 and is likely higher today[^89]. Smaller annoyances have their own fixes: wrapping cords with duct tape usually leaves a sticky residue, but cutting a strip so only the two ends stay sticky and folding the rest onto itself — similar to a theme-park wristband — creates a strong, non-sticky wrap[^91]. Even something as trivial as deflating an inflatable pool toy is easier with a household item, since sticking a drinking straw into the valve holds it open and lets the air escape far faster than pinching it by hand[^92].

### Home Security and Unexpected Visitors
Homes have vulnerabilities and surprises that are worth knowing how to handle. Garage doors with emergency release latches can be opened from outside by threading a coat hanger or a hooked piece of wire through the top of the door to trip the latch (or pull the rope many latches have) — a trick worth knowing both for letting yourself back in and for understanding how someone else could get in the same way[^93]. Wildlife occasionally finds its way indoors too, and knowing how to quickly and calmly remove a bat that's gotten into your home helps turn a startling situation — bats hovering overhead or hanging from doorframes — into a manageable one[^94].

### Moving, Renting, and Work-Life Balance at Home
Finding and evaluating a place to live is its own skill set. When comparing apartment search and rental listing services, the "best" one isn't necessarily the one with the most listings — it might be the one that presents results most usefully, filters by your preferences, lets you apply directly, or simply surfaces new listings the fastest[^95]. And home life isn't only about the physical space — it's also about actually being present in it. A short talk from developer/designer Pam Selle argued that leaving work on time comes down to remembering a few key things, chief among them that time is money, so working extra unpaid hours effectively means earning less per hour worked[^97].

## Food & Cooking

### Kitchen Techniques & Tricks
A handful of small technique tweaks can make a big difference in the kitchen. When making onion rings, the trick to a satisfying crunch is stirring a bit of instant mashed potato flakes into the tempura-style batter before dredging the onions, which thickens the coating and adds extra snap[^98]. Serving pie cleanly has its own workaround: instead of fighting with the first slice, cut a small "sacrificial" sliver first, which opens up enough room to get a knife or pie server underneath the remaining slices and lift them out intact[^99]. For a campfire dessert, hollow out an orange, fill it partway with cake batter, wrap it in foil, and let it rest on the coals for about half an hour for a single-serving cake baked right inside the peel[^100]. And for cooks looking to vary their weeknight routine, a chart built around 36 world cuisines suggests just three characteristic spices per region, letting something as ordinary as meatloaf take on the flavor profile of Moroccan or other regional cooking[^101].

### Eggs (and Egg Cartons)
Eggs get their own set of workarounds. Boiling a large batch at once risks cracked shells from eggs knocking against each other, but wrapping the eggs in a dish towel—layered between the eggs in the pot—cushions them so they can be boiled in bulk without damage[^102]. Once boiled, peeling can be simplified with nothing more than a teaspoon, maneuvered to pop the shell off in one piece rather than picking at fragments[^103]. Yolks get a second life too: after making cocktails that call for egg whites, the leftover yolks can be cured, then grated over salads, pizza, pasta, or deviled eggs, where the concentrated, savory cured yolk intensifies the classic filling[^104]. Even the carton eggs come in has a use beyond the kitchen—flipped upside down, empty egg cartons make an improvised, if unattractive, laptop stand that lets air circulate underneath a hot laptop instead of trapping heat against your legs[^105].

### Drinks & Bar Tricks
Several tips revolve around drinks and bar culture. Beer Menus is a site (with a mobile-friendly version) that lets beer fans search bars nationwide to see what's on tap or in bottles and cans, along with pricing, after expanding beyond its original New York City coverage[^106]. In the field, a bottle opener isn't strictly necessary: wrapping a hand around the cap and using a spoon's curved handle as leverage against the lip of the bottle can pop the top off in a pinch, similar to the butter-knife method[^107]. Getting a lime wedge into a beer bottle is normally messy, but sliding it in with the flat side of a knife channels the juice into the bottle cleanly instead of leaving it stuck in the neck—a method that requires a steady, careful hand[^108]. Pouring liquids has its own fix, too: turning a juice carton upside down before pouring reduces the glugging and splashing that comes from pouring it right-side up, a trick that also applies to any screw-cap carton, including motor oil[^109]. On the health side, an infographic lays out just how many calories alcohol adds to a diet, noting that a frozen margarita can rival a cheeseburger in calories and a glass of wine can be comparable to a slice of cake, while recommending better mixers like tonic water over sugary ones[^110]. And when liquor stores and grocery delivery slots became hard to come by, local breweries emerged as an alternative source for getting alcohol delivered directly, filling a gap left by regions where grocery and liquor delivery weren't available[^111].

### Comfort Food Favorites
Some entries celebrate favorite comfort foods and creative riffs on them. The burrito gets singled out as a nearly perfect food, its appeal coming from how a simple tortilla wrapped around a mix of regional ingredients can be endlessly varied while still holding together as a cohesive, portable meal[^112]. In a similar spirit of reinvention, a beloved sandwich—the Italian sub, grinder, or hoagie—gets transformed into a hot casserole, turning a cold-cut favorite into a comforting baked dish[^113]. And for a frozen treat with a healthier bent, baking a sweet potato and then freezing it results in a naturally sweet, incidentally vegan popsicle, an idea inspired by a chef's social media posts showcasing the technique[^114].

### Grocery Shopping & Food Science
The last cluster covers the science and logistics around food and shopping. A 2014 Consumer Reports chart ranked fish by mercury risk, identifying shellfish like shrimp, scallops, and oysters, along with wild salmon and tilapia, as generally low-mercury choices, and argued for eating even less tuna than the FDA and EPA guidelines of that time recommended—a concern especially relevant for pregnant or nursing women, though FDA/EPA fish advice has been revised since then, so it's worth checking their current guidance for specific serving recommendations[^115]. Airline food's notorious blandness has a scientific explanation as well: taste and smell both depend on humidity to function well, and the dry, pressurized cabin environment dulls the senses needed to fully enjoy a meal, whether it's the in-flight service or snacks packed from home[^116]. Grocery shopping itself became a challenge worth solving with technology: a web-based notification tool was built to alert shoppers by text when a curbside pickup slot opened up, sparing them from having to repeatedly refresh their cart to snag a delivery window[^117]. 

## Health, Diet & Fitness

### Running and Exercise Routines
Getting into an exercise habit doesn't have to mean an hour at the gym: a daily 20-minute plan built with input from trainers and fitness experts is designed to keep the body active and growing stronger without heavy equipment, varying the routine day to day to stave off boredom[^119]. 

Running in particular comes up repeatedly as an accessible, low-cost way to start a fitness routine. One piece addresses the specific fear of being "bad" at running—the kind of out-of-breath, doubled-over experience that keeps people from lacing up in the first place—and lays out how to begin anyway[^121]. Another, written by a Lifehacker staffer reflecting on her own return to training after running the NYC marathon, argues that the real secret to becoming a runner is building a consistent routine, so that pulling on running shoes stops being something to think twice about[^122]. Not every path marketed as a fitness shortcut holds up, though: time in a sauna can feel good, but on its own it isn't a substitute for exercise, and it doesn't detox the body or melt away fat[^123].

### Muscle Recovery and Body Mechanics
Some exercises earn their keep specifically by protecting against pain elsewhere in the body: the bird dog exercise works the stabilizer muscles in the core, encouraging people to move from the hips rather than the lower back, which helps reduce lower back pain—even though it looks deceptively easy and is commonly performed with poor form[^124]. Understanding what happens after a hard workout matters too. The soreness that sets in a day or two later, known as delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), isn't caused by lactic acid or toxins, as is commonly assumed; if it were, it would show up during the workout itself rather than hours or days afterward, and exactly what does cause it is still not fully understood[^125]. In the moment, a different kind of muscle pain—a sudden cramp, like the calf cramp that wakes someone up at night or stops them mid-workout—can often be released by contracting the opposing muscle, such as pulling the toes upward to counter a calf cramp, rather than stretching the cramping muscle itself[^126].

### Mental Health and Stress Management
Managing stress sometimes comes down to a shift in perspective. Jerry Seinfeld has described calming his nerves during high-pressure moments in the Seinfeld writers' room by looking at Hubble Space Telescope photographs, using the vastness of space to remind himself how insignificant any single stressful moment really is[^127]. Screen habits factor into mental health too: researchers at Ohio State University Medical Center linked late-night sessions in front of a glowing screen in the dark to changes in behavior and the brain resembling depression, based on how hamsters responded to dim, unnatural light at night, suggesting that either stepping away from the computer or turning on the lights during those sessions could help[^128].

### Pandemic-Era Health and Safety
The COVID-19 pandemic reshaped both the stresses people faced and the scams built to exploit them. With burnout described as "chronic stress gone awry" by psychologist Sheryl Ziegler, prolonged periods of high stress—like the nonstop barrage many people faced during the COVID-19 pandemic—can push people toward emotional, mental, and physical exhaustion, and recognizing the early signs can help head off burnout before it fully sets in[^129]. 

### Everyday Health Tips and Quick Fixes
Smaller, practical fixes round out the picture. Blocked sinuses can be an annoyance any time of year—and especially frustrating when a sniffle leaves you second-guessing whether it's allergies or something else—but they can sometimes be relieved in about 20 seconds using an acupressure trick involving the face and tongue, offering an alternative to reaching for a pill[^131]. Excessive sweating, meanwhile, can have a genetic component according to dermatologist Michele Green, but there are still ways to manage it even for people who sweat heavily in everyday situations like job interviews[^132]. And health isn't only a matter of the human body: Apple updated its AppleCare terms so that batteries in iPhones, iPads, iPods, Apple Watches, and Macs became eligible for replacement once they fell below 80% of their original capacity, a looser threshold than the previous 50% cutoff that had applied to iOS devices[^133].

## Relationships & Social Life

### Dating and Meeting People
Modern dating largely runs through apps now, and the options split along fairly distinct lines: Tinder skews toward casual hookups, eharmony leans intense and marriage-minded, and Hinge is built around personality-driven prompts rather than just photos, so picking the right platform is mostly about matching the app's crowd to what you're actually looking for[^134]. Once a conversation is actually happening, small talk can still be the hard part — comedian Jerry Seinfeld's trick, shared on his web series *Comedians in Cars Getting Coffee*, is to lean on questions with numerical answers ("How long have you lived here?" "What time do you start work?") because they tend to have a concrete answer and can help keep the exchange moving[^135]. Before that first date even happens, a bit of online background-checking has become normal practice: part of what makes profile prompts on apps like Hinge useful is that they give you a jumping-off point for small talk, and a reasonable amount of pre-date "stalking" covers basic safety and vibe-checking without crossing into overkill[^136]. And once you're actually dating, physical differences like a big height gap between partners are mostly a non-issue in the grand scheme of things — the main advice is simply not to be weird about it, even though notably short or tall daters will likely run into some stereotyping along the way[^137].

### Sustaining Relationships and Staying Connected
Distance and conflict are two of the biggest pressures relationships face, but neither has to be fatal. A study published in the *Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy*, led by researcher Emma Dargie, found that long-distance couples were often happier than proximate ones, suggesting that raw physical distance doesn't doom a relationship — what matters more is the gap between your expectations for the relationship and the reality of your situation, and getting aligned on that together[^138]. Closer to home, it's worth being able to tell the difference between an ordinary rough patch and a genuinely dysfunctional dynamic: a healthy relationship is supposed to be stable and nurturing, giving both partners a source of support and celebration, so a bad fight is worth examining for whether it reflects a bad day or a deeper, more serious pattern[^139]. For parents, keeping the relationship itself alive can come down to logistics — a "sleepover swap," where you team up with another family and hire one babysitter to watch both sets of kids during a sleepover, splits the cost of date night and makes it easier to actually get out of the house[^140]. And connection doesn't have to be limited to the people already in your life: during the isolation of early lockdown, when Zoom calls and FaceTime covered the basics but travel and new encounters felt out of reach, getting an international pen pal was offered as a way to stay curious about and connected to the wider world[^141].

## Travel

### Packing and Carry-On Strategy
Packing well is a skill that improves with repetition: military packing tips gathered from soldiers, airmen, and sailors emphasize practicing packing, unpacking, and repacking your bag several times before a trip so you get faster at it and memorize exactly where everything lives[^142]. That same discipline pays off when trying to avoid checked luggage altogether — traveling with only a carry-on means deciding carefully what to bring, which cuts down on baggage fees, the risk of lost or damaged bags, and unnecessary contact with germy conveyor belts and cargo holds[^143]. Bulkier items pose their own challenge: a formal jacket or blazer can be folded into a compact roll using a step-by-step technique that turns one sleeve inside out and tucks the garment into itself so it keeps its shape inside a carry-on[^144]. Carry-on packing isn't only about clothes, either — travelers who like a drink in the air can build a personal cocktail kit with travel-size bottles, citrus, and mixers to bring along, since airplane bars offer limited options[^145]. For frequent travelers heading to the same destination repeatedly, one alternative to packing (and checking) a bag every trip is "travel caching," a tactic popularized by Tim Ferriss in which a suitcase or trunk of clothes and supplies is stored permanently at a regularly visited hotel or location, borrowing from the idea of urban geocaching[^146].

### Booking and Flying
Knowing airline passenger rights can prevent getting shortchanged: after a 2011 update to the Department of Transportation's flyer's rights rules, airlines became required to reimburse passengers for lost baggage and to compensate bumped passengers up to twice the ticket price (as much as $800 at the time) for certain delays — DOT periodically raises these caps (they rose again to $1,075–$2,150 effective January 2025), so check the current DOT denied-boarding rules rather than relying on the 2011 dollar figure[^147]. That compensation math matters especially when a flight is overbooked — passengers are generally better off declining an airline's voluntary bump offer (often just $200-$400 in cash or vouchers) and instead waiting to be involuntarily denied boarding, since federal regulations at the time (2015) required carriers to pay out far more, up to $1,300 depending on the delay — those caps have risen further since (DOT raised them again effective January 2025), so confirm the current limits rather than counting on this specific figure[^148]. Timing can matter, though the old "book on a Tuesday" advice has been debunked by more recent data — fares now shift constantly via dynamic pricing, and studies since have found day-of-booking savings are minimal. What holds up better is the day you actually fly: one 2016 analysis of summer flight data found Tuesdays and Wednesdays were the cheapest days to travel, with July the most expensive month and Sunday travel commanding a premium over the same trip on a Tuesday — treat this as a rough seasonal pattern rather than a reliable rule, since pricing algorithms have grown more dynamic since the analysis was done[^149].

### Hotel Stays
Where you end up sleeping can depend on more than luck. A hotel front desk supervisor explained that the worst rooms are often assigned to bookings made through third-party sites like Expedia or handed out at random, which is useful to know when trying to avoid a bad room assignment[^150]. Once checked in, travelers shouldn't assume they're stuck with whatever the hotel delivers: if advertised amenities like a pool, spa, or gym turn out to be closed or unavailable, guests can ask the hotel for a refund, room credit, or even an upgrade to make up for the shortfall[^151]. Hotels can also be enjoyed without booking a room at all — some properties let non-guests pay for day access to pools, spas, and restaurants in a practice known as "daycationing," a lower-cost way to feel like you're on vacation while staying local[^152]. And a small trick from a British Airways flight attendant helps prevent one of the most common hotel-safe mistakes: place a single travel shoe in the safe along with your passport and valuables, so that when getting dressed to leave you'll notice the missing shoe and remember what else is locked inside[^153].

### Alternative Lodging and Ground Transport
Beyond traditional hotels, vacation rentals offer another lodging option: the metasearch site Tripping.com (still active) lets travelers search multiple rental platforms — including Vrbo (which absorbed the once-separate HomeAway brand in 2019-2020), FlipKey, and Booking.com (though not Airbnb) — at once instead of checking each one individually[^154]. For getting around without owning a car or paying full rental rates, TransferCar lists one-way rental cars and RVs that need to be relocated between cities, sometimes bundling perks like free gas, other expenses, or insurance coverage for drivers willing to take on the route[^155].

### Documents and Trip-Prep Tools
 Trip prep sometimes extends to outfitting the device you're traveling with, too: a 2012 roundup of recommended Mac software for students leaving for school covered note-taking apps like Evernote, OneNote, Simplenote, and Google Keep for getting a new laptop set up before heading out — the specific app lineup is dated, though the underlying idea of picking a note-taking app before school starts still holds[^157].

## Technology & Digital Life

### Mac and OS X Tips and Tricks
Mac power users have long relied on launcher utilities to keep their hands on the keyboard, and Alfred is one of the most popular: it runs quietly in the background and pops up with a keyboard shortcut, letting you find applications and files, do calculations, look up definitions, view clipboard history, and dig up contact info, all from a few keystrokes[^158]. Under the hood, OS X also ships with a set of Mac-only, text-based Terminal utilities that aren't found on other operating systems, and learning them can help power users bridge the gap between UNIX habits and native Mac tools[^159]. More broadly, a good chunk of everyday computing tasks are simply better handled from the command line than through a GUI, and Mac users can run many of the same Linux-centric command-line tools in Terminal that Windows users have historically reached for via Cygwin—though Windows users today are more likely to use the built-in Windows Subsystem for Linux (WSL) for the same purpose[^160]. Setting up a new Mac is famously tedious, but Homebrew—which has since folded the old separate "Homebrew Cask" project into its core `brew install --cask` command—can automate the whole process of finding and installing software with a single Terminal command instead of hunting down, downloading, and running installers one by one[^161]. 

Several tips address small, often-overlooked Finder and Dock settings. By default, Finder's list view only shows individual file sizes, not folder sizes, but switching to list view, opening Show View Options, and checking "Calculate all sizes" reveals the full size of every folder[^163]. When copying files into a folder that already contains files with the same name, holding the Option key while the Skip/Stop/Replace dialog is open turns the Skip button into a hidden "Keep Both" button so you can retain both the old and new versions[^164]. Users who keep their Dock auto-hidden can also eliminate the roughly half-second delay before it appears by mouse-over, using a Terminal command that works specifically in OS X Lion[^165].

Mac startup itself has a whole set of hidden options: on Intel Macs, holding down particular key combinations immediately after powering on can start the Mac in modes like Safe Mode (via Shift), which loads only the minimum necessary kernel extensions and disables startup items, user-installed fonts, and font caches for troubleshooting—Apple Silicon Macs (M1 and later) instead use a startup-options screen accessed by holding the power button, so the specific key combinations no longer apply[^166]. For backups, Time Machine is a built-in macOS service that makes the process easy, and as an alternative to Apple's official Time Capsule—which Apple discontinued in 2018 and no longer sells—a guide shows how to turn a Raspberry Pi into a low-powered, Time Machine-compatible network-attached storage device, complete with encryption and scheduled backups[^168].

OS X also keeps logs that many users don't realize exist. It records essentially everything downloaded to the Mac, and a specific Terminal command run against a SQLite database in `~/Library/Preferences` can display that entire download history—on current macOS, Terminal will likely need to be granted "Full Disk Access" in System Settings > Privacy & Security for this to work, a permission system that didn't exist when this tip was written[^169]; a companion tip explains how to clear that same download log out if you'd rather not keep it around, though on current macOS this also likely requires granting Terminal "Full Disk Access" first[^170]. Beyond downloads, a Mac's network hardware address (MAC address) can be used to track a machine, and a bash script can randomize that address every 30 seconds to make tracking harder, though it was written and tested specifically for older versions of OS X—current macOS (since Sequoia) now randomizes Wi-Fi addresses automatically by default, and System Integrity Protection may block this kind of manual script from working on modern Macs[^171]. Finally, for anyone annoyed by a steady stream of Notification Center birthday reminders, opening Calendar, right-clicking the birthday calendar, choosing "Get Info," and checking "Ignore Alerts" turns them off—along with doing the same for any duplicate birthday calendars synced from Facebook or Google[^172].

## Privacy & Security

### Browser and Search Privacy
DuckDuckGo built its reputation as a tracker-free alternative to Google search, offering browsing utilities designed to minimize the number of sites and companies tracking a person across the web, and at the time offered settings that could be customized to different levels of protection depending on how much a user wanted to pull back from Google, Facebook, Twitter, and Amazon — DuckDuckGo's specific tools and protections have changed considerably since, so check its current privacy features rather than relying on this list[^235]. On the Mac side, setting up a new machine or a fresh install of macOS is still a good moment to check privacy settings, since the operating system keeps a lot of activity behind the scenes and even simple things like text messages can pop up where someone else can see them if settings aren't adjusted — note that the specific menus have moved since this was written (System Preferences is now System Settings, with a renamed Privacy & Security pane)[^237]. 

### Tracking by Social Platforms and Email
 Tracking isn't limited to the web and social apps, either: email marketers use techniques like tracking pixels to learn whether and when a message was opened, and while Apple's Mail Privacy Protection now blocks most of this automatically by default on iPhone, iPad, and Mac, it's worth confirming the setting is still turned on[^243].

### Public Wi-Fi, Encrypted Connections, and Anonymity Tools
 BitTorrent was, at the time, still one of the more capable file-sharing tools around, and getting the most out of it meant paying attention to both download speed and keeping activity private from prying eyes — the specific client settings referenced are dated, so check current guidance for today's torrent clients[^245]. A flash drive could double as a portable privacy toolkit for situations like using an internet café, tunneling back to a home computer from a cubicle, or avoiding leaving traces on a borrowed computer, built around SSH-friendly portable applications and secure connections — a niche, dated approach now that smartphones and consumer VPN apps cover most of these scenarios more easily[^246]. Whether a VPN actually hides browsing activity from an employer is a more nuanced question, with the simplest advice being to keep work and personal browsing separate entirely — no BitTorrent on a work laptop, no browsing sensitive material over the company network or VPN, and no sending anything questionable through a work email account[^247].

### Device, Account, and Financial Security
Hardening a Mac involves treating the Mac's settings app (System Preferences at the time of writing, renamed System Settings since macOS Ventura) as a frequent stop, since Apple regularly ships security updates and patches for new threats, making it important to understand the reasoning behind each recommended tweak rather than just applying settings blindly[^248]. On the financial side, a long history of breaches at major companies — Experian, Facebook, Google, and Equifax among them, with many more since — makes the case for setting up financial accounts as though a hack is inevitable, starting with the basic but often-skipped step of creating a genuinely strong password[^249]. A broader self-assessment breaks online security into four areas — passwords, browsers, home Wi-Fi and networking, and public Wi-Fi browsing — with a checklist ranging from bare-minimum precautions to more paranoid, "tin-foil-hat" measures in each category; the framework still holds up, but the specific tools and settings it recommends are from 2012 and should be checked against current options[^251]. Physical security got its own twist when it emerged that an iPhone case with a built-in infrared camera could read the thermal heat signature left on an ATM keypad or a numeric door lock after someone typed in their PIN or combination, a risk that can be defeated with a simple trick to avoid leaving a heat trace[^253]. And when applying for a job, it's worth holding back a Social Security number until there's an actual formal offer in hand, since basic information like employment history is normal for an application but a request for a SSN before an offer is a red flag worth treating with suspicion[^254].

### Disposable Identities and Private Access
For situations that call for keeping a real phone number out of someone else's hands — texting a potential roommate found on Craigslist, messaging a stranger buying an old PC on eBay, or fielding calls from an ex — the iPhone app Burner generated short-term, disposable phone numbers for calls and texts and masked outgoing communication behind that number[^255].

For a different kind of build, instructions from World Paper Airplane Association record holder John Collins — "The Paper Airplane Guy" — lay out how to fold the design that, thrown by former arena football quarterback Joe Ayoob, set the world distance record in 2012 (since surpassed by other teams), using nothing more than a single sheet of paper[^266]. ### Everyday Diversions

## Miscellaneous Lifehacks

### Quick Physical Tricks and DIY Fixes
A lot of the grab-bag tips in this collection are small physical maneuvers that solve an everyday annoyance with almost no materials. The microwave, it turns out, is more versatile than most people give it credit for — a roundup of tricks covers steaming vegetables in a covered bowl with a splash of water, among other uses that go beyond simple reheating[^270]. For photos, ditching the word "cheese" in favor of "cheeks" is offered as a way to produce a more natural, Duchenne-style smile, since the "k" sound shapes the mouth and cheeks into a more genuine expression[^271]. Glasses-wearers get a fix for perpetual fogging: rubbing a small amount of shaving cream on the lenses, a trick originally shared for cold-weather mirror fogging that took on new relevance once face masks became an everyday accessory[^272]. When it comes to opening a bottle with no opener in sight, hammering a construction nail into a piece of wood at roughly a 45-degree angle creates an improvised bottle opener, as long as the nail head is wide enough to grip the cap[^273]. Hanging art evenly is another common headache solved with an ordinary object — sliding a fork onto the nail, with the handle angled upward and the tines gripping the shaft, gives the picture wire a stable perch to hook onto so the piece hangs straight without any measuring[^274]. Doors that slam can be quieted (though not fully silenced) by sticking felt pads inside the door frame near the latch or along the top and bottom edges, catching the door before it hits the frame[^275].

Several of these fixes lean further into DIY territory. A functional tablet stylus can be built from an arrowhead pencil eraser, a metal paintbrush, and some craft foam to hold the pieces together[^276]. On the more ambitious end, a serviceable air purifier can be built for around $30 by taping a MERV-13-rated furnace filter to a 20-inch box fan, a setup demonstrated to remove roughly 90 percent of the smoke, dust, and allergen particles a much pricier HEPA purifier would catch[^277]. For looking sharp instead of rumpled, the "military tuck" involves pinching the excess fabric at a shirt's side seams and folding it back before tucking it in, producing a slimmer line than a plain tuck[^278]. And when shipping something breakable, marking the box "glass" or "liquid" is suggested as more effective than "fragile," since handlers reportedly respond to the concrete threat of a mess to clean up rather than a vague warning label[^279].

### Safety, Prep, and Everyday Maintenance
A handful of items are about being ready for ordinary mishaps rather than performing a trick. A graphic on tire safety lays out how to check tread depth with a coin, when to rotate tires, how to read a tire's sidewall, and where to check inflation, all framed as routine maintenance that affects both gas mileage and accident risk[^280]. In a similar vein, an infographic designed to be saved to a phone or printed and kept in a wallet walks through what to do in the event of an emergency — such as getting pulled over, being mugged, or being in an accident — on the logic that this kind of guidance is only useful if it's on hand when needed[^281]. Paper records get their own cautionary note: scanning receipts into a document-management service is a good way to keep them backed up, but it's worth holding onto the originals for a while too, since some stores and store employees have been known to refuse a photocopied receipt for a return even when a photo on a phone eventually persuades them[^282]. On the housekeeping side, a roundup of chores that only need to happen about once a year — as opposed to the daily grind of dishes and floors — is offered as a small consolation for anyone tracking a home-maintenance list[^283].

### Communication and Social Savvy
Several items focus on the small verbal and behavioral choices that shape how other people perceive you. Borrowing a rule from improv comedy, one tip suggests that when a conversation stalls, falling back on the "history/philosophy/metaphor" approach — bringing up the history of a topic, a broader philosophical angle, or an apt metaphor — can keep a scene, and a conversation, moving instead of stalling into awkward silence[^284]. Relatedly, instead of reflexively answering "I'm busy" when asked how you've been, a productivity author argues that busyness has become a kind of humble-brag rather than a real indicator of anything, and that naming what you're actually accomplishing lands better than flaunting how overloaded you are[^285]. A study on written communication found that including a middle initial — as in "David F. Clark" rather than "David Clark" — measurably increased how intelligent and high-status readers judged a piece of writing to be, though the effect only showed up in settings tied to intellectual performance[^286]. Typing habits reveal generational history too: people taught to type on a typewriter were generally told to put two spaces after a period, but a career counselor's advice frames single-spacing as the modern standard, warning that the old habit can read as an age signal on a resume or in professional emails[^287].

Workplace and social dynamics get their own set of tips. Heading into a job interview, it's suggested that candidates come prepared with their own questions about a prospective employer's culture rather than only fielding questions from the hiring side, since the interview format can otherwise turn into something closer to a one-sided pitch[^288]. For anyone in a position to impose change on other people — a manager redirecting a team, or a parent moving a family — leaving room for some kind of choice within the mandatory shift, even a small one, is described as a way to make people feel like participants in the change rather than subjects of it[^289]. And one more pointed workplace hack: creating an email persona for a fictitious male assistant, a workaround one writer's friend used after growing tired of being labeled "difficult" or "bossy" for tasks that read differently when a man's name is attached to them[^290].

### Mindset, Motivation, and Self-Improvement
A few entries are less about a discrete action and more about reframing how to think about setbacks, personality, and time. One roundup collects stories of well-known people's biggest professional failures and how they recovered from them, framed as a reminder that failure is a normal part of any path to success rather than a sign that a career is over[^291]. Relatedly, a piece on channeling one's "dark side" argues that traits like envy don't have to be purely destructive — distinguishing "benign" envy, which reflects your own aspirations, from "malicious" envy, is offered as a first step toward turning a negative feeling into motivation or insight[^292]. A podcast conversation with journalist Celeste Headlee, author of "Do Nothing," tackles the broader culture of busyness and overwork, discussing why so many people end up working more rather than less even while at home, and what that does to both mind and body[^293]. And for anyone trying to get through more reading in less time, the "third word rule" proposes skipping the first and last couple of words on each line, relying on peripheral vision to still catch the content — a technique said to work even better when text is formatted into narrower columns[^294].
